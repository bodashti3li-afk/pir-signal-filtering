The Sensor

What is a PIR sensor?

A passive infrared (PIR) sensor detects the heat signature of objects within its field of view. It does not emit any energy. It only reads the infrared radiation already present in the environment.

  • Two pyroelectric elements are wired differentially behind a Fresnel lens.
  • The lens divides the field into discrete zones. As a warm body crosses zones, the IR power on each element changes by a different amount.
  • The output is a small AC signal whose shape and polarity depend on the direction, distance, and speed of motion.
  • Before amplification, this signal is typically in the microvolt range.

Where it is used

Why PIR sensors matter

Because they are passive, low-power, and inexpensive, PIR sensors appear in nearly every system that needs to detect human presence without using a camera.

  • Security alarms. Intruder detection in dark or unmonitored areas, where vision-based systems are impractical.
  • Automatic lighting. Lamps activate only when occupancy is detected, reducing energy use.
  • Smart buildings. Occupancy estimation drives HVAC scheduling and overall energy management.
  • Healthcare monitoring. Tracks activity of elderly patients in their homes without recording video, preserving privacy.

Why filtering is needed

What contaminates the signal

The raw output of a PIR sensor is mixed with several non-motion signals. Each one occupies a different frequency range, and any of them can cause false detections if not removed.

Below 0.5 Hz
Slow thermal drift
Sunlight crossing a floor, HVAC airflow, gradual room warming. Produces a slow baseline shift that the comparator can mistake for motion.
50 Hz & above
Electrical interference
Mains coupling at 50 Hz, switching converters, RF emissions, motor PWM. Couples into the analog front-end through power lines or stray capacitance.
  • Without filtering, the system either triggers on conditions that are not motion, or raises its threshold and misses real events.

The motion band

Where motion lives in frequency

When a person walks across the Fresnel zones, the rate of IR change at the sensor falls between 0.1 Hz and 5 Hz. Texas Instruments cites a typical detection band of 0.7 – 30 Hz, with most indoor systems focused on the lower portion.

0.1 – 5 Hz

> 1 kHz

  • The design objective: a band-pass response that passes 0.1 – 5 Hz and attenuates everything outside it.

RC low-pass design

Sizing the capacitor

For a passive RC low-pass filter, the cutoff frequency is set by the product of the resistance and the capacitance.

fc = 1 / ( 2π · R · C )
Given R = 250 kΩ and fc = 5 Hz:
C = 1 / ( 2π × 250 000 × 5 )
C = 1.27 × 10⁻⁷ F
Capacitor value
C = 127 nF
  • At 100 Hz (typical fan-induced ripple) the output is reduced by approximately a factor of twenty relative to the input.
  • The same equation, applied with a larger capacitor, sizes the high-pass stage for a 0.1 Hz cutoff.

Beyond simple RC

Improving on the basic chain

In demanding environments, simple passive filters are not sufficient. Modern PIR systems combine analog refinement with digital signal processing for better selectivity and adaptability.

Analog · physical
Differential sensing
The two pyroelectric elements are wired against each other. Common-mode IR changes (background warming, sunlight) cancel out; only differential changes caused by motion across zones produce an output.
Digital · DSP
Wavelet & adaptive filters
Wavelet decomposition analyzes the signal in time and frequency at the same time. Adaptive filters update their coefficients based on the noise environment, useful for non-stationary interference.
  • Active multi-stage op-amp filters provide gain (typically ×1000) along with selective filtering, since the raw signal is too small for direct sampling.

008AI in PIR Noise RemovalPD3
Intelligent classification

Learning what real motion looks like

Threshold-based detection cannot distinguish a person walking from a curtain moving in a draft. Machine learning models, trained on labeled waveforms, can.

  • Neural networks classify the signal by its waveform shape rather than its amplitude alone, identifying motion patterns that fixed thresholds miss.
  • Smart buildings. ML-based PIR systems estimate occupancy counts and recognize specific activities, feeding directly into energy management algorithms (Shokrollahi et al., 2024).
  • Fire detection. Deep learning combined with wavelet feature extraction identifies early heat signatures that simple thresholds miss (Xavier & Nanayakkara, 2022).
  • Healthcare. Systems learn the daily routine of an individual and flag anomalies such as falls, all without using a camera.

Summary

Conclusion

The raw PIR output is a few microvolts of useful motion mixed with thermal drift, mains interference, and switching noise. Filtering is what makes the sensor reliable.
  • A high-pass filter at 0.1 Hz removes slow thermal drift before amplification.
  • A low-pass filter at 5 Hz removes mains coupling and high-frequency electrical noise.
  • Together they produce a band-pass response tuned to the 0.1 – 5 Hz human motion band.
  • Modern systems extend this baseline with differential sensing, wavelet transforms, and machine learning for non-stationary environments.
